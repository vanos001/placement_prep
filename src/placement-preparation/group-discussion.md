# Group Discussion Preparation

A group discussion evaluates structured thinking, listening, collaboration, and
clarity under time pressure. The goal is not to speak the most; it is to help
the group reach a better, evidence-based conclusion.

## A practical structure

### First minute: frame the question

Define the terms, state the scope, and separate facts from assumptions. If the
topic is broad, propose two or three dimensions such as economic, social,
technical, ethical, or implementation impact.

### Middle: add and connect ideas

Make one clear point at a time. Build on another speaker’s idea, ask a useful
question, or introduce a counterexample. Use examples rather than slogans.

### Final: synthesize

Summarize the strongest arguments on both sides, identify conditions under
which each applies, and suggest a balanced conclusion or next step.

## Useful sentence patterns

- “I agree with the goal, and I would add one implementation constraint…”
- “There are two different questions here: short-term impact and long-term…”
- “Can we distinguish the evidence from the assumption in that claim?”
- “Let me summarize the points so far before we choose a conclusion.”
- “A counterexample is…, so I would qualify the original statement.”

## Behaviors to demonstrate

- Listen without interrupting.
- Invite a quiet participant into the conversation.
- Disagree with reasoning, never with a person.
- Keep the discussion on the question.
- Use a time check and help the group converge.
- Admit uncertainty and propose how to verify it.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Speaking repeatedly without advancing the argument.
- Treating confidence or volume as evidence.
- Interrupting, dismissing, or personally attacking another speaker.
- Changing the topic when challenged.
- Giving a long monologue without a conclusion.
- Refusing to summarize because the group did not reach perfect agreement.

## Practice format

For a ten-minute practice:

1. Two minutes: each participant frames the topic.
2. Five minutes: exchange arguments and counterexamples.
3. Two minutes: agree on a synthesis.
4. One minute: each participant gives feedback on listening, evidence, and
   clarity.

Record whether the conclusion changed when new evidence appeared. That is a
better collaboration signal than whether one person “won.”
